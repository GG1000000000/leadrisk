How to read it

The United States banned lead in house paint in 1978. The older the home, the more likely lead paint is somewhere in it. A national survey of US homes measured how often, by the year the home was built. The first three figures are the survey's; the fourth is the value this tool uses, since the surveys' own figure for newer homes, about 7 in 100, is mostly lead glaze on ceramic tile rather than wall paint:

87 in 100built before 1940
69 in 100built 1940 to 1959
24 in 100built 1960 to 1977
3 in 100built 1978 or later

These are odds, not an inspection. A home built in 1925 may have been fully stripped and repainted; a home built in 1970 may still have the original windows. What the records can tell you is whether it is worth looking.

Lead paint that is intact and undisturbed is usually not the problem. The problem is paint that chips, peels or rubs into dust, which happens most at windows, doors and stairs, and during any sanding or renovation. Children under six are the most at risk, because they put hands and objects in their mouths and because lead does the most harm to a developing brain. Lead also crosses the placenta, so pregnancy is the other time to be careful. There is no known safe level.

If the home is from before 1978

  • Get your child's blood lead tested. Ask your pediatrician or clinic. Medicaid covers the test and requires it at 12 and 24 months. Many state and local health departments test for free.
  • Look at the paint. Chipping, peeling or chalking paint, and bare soil next to the house, are the things to fix first. Wet-wipe window sills and floors; dry sweeping spreads lead dust.
  • Before any renovation, sanding or scraping, use a contractor certified under EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, or learn the lead-safe methods yourself. Dry sanding or scraping old paint spreads lead dust through the whole house. In New York State's investigations of children with high blood lead, renovation or repair work turned up in about one case in seven, and most of that work was done by the residents themselves (CDC, 2009).
  • For a definitive answer, a certified lead inspector or risk assessor can test the paint, dust and soil. Your state or local health department can tell you who is certified and whether there is money to help with repairs.
  • Questions: the National Lead Information Center, 1-800-424-LEAD (5323), run by EPA.

If the home is from 1978 or later

Paint is rarely the issue. Lead can still arrive other ways: older plumbing and solder, soil near old buildings or roads, some imported ceramics, cosmetics, spices and toys, and work clothes from jobs that handle lead. A blood test is still the right call if you have any doubt.

Where the data comes from

The house lookup reads year-built records that county and state assessors publish, gathered into one database: about 48.6 million property records from 119 public GIS sources across 41 states and DC. Each result shows which source it came from and the parcel number, so it can be checked against the county's own record.

The ZIP lookup uses the Census Bureau's American Community Survey: the number of homes in each ZIP code built in each era, and the poverty rate. The share expected to contain lead paint applies the survey odds above to those counts. The Census groups homes as built before 1940, 1940 to 1979, and 1980 or later, so the middle group gets a blended rate of 45 in 100 and the last gets 3 in 100.

The odds by era come from HUD's National Survey of Lead and Allergens in Housing, which tested paint in a national sample of 831 homes in 1998 to 2000 (Jacobs et al., Environmental Health Perspectives, 2002, doi:10.1289/ehp.021100599). HUD's two later American Healthy Homes Surveys (2005 to 2006 and 2018 to 2019) found nearly the same pattern: 86 and 85 in 100 before 1940, 66 and 61 for 1940 to 1959, 25 and 24 for 1960 to 1977.

The method and its checks are published in two open-access papers in GeoHealth (American Geophysical Union, 2026). The first built the national map and tested it against measured blood lead in children in eleven states: doi:10.1029/2026GH002053. The second asked what address-level records add on top of neighborhood-level screening: doi:10.1029/2026GH002142. The full dataset and code are on Zenodo: doi:10.5281/zenodo.21685187. EPA researchers working independently reached a similar result at the neighborhood level across five states: the share of homes built before 1940 was the most consistently significant predictor of children's blood lead, holding in every state they modeled (Dietrich et al., ACS Environmental Au, 2026, doi:10.1021/acsenvironau.5c00104).

What it can't do

  • Nine states are not covered yet (their statewide parcel layers carry no year-built field, or the year-built data is gated or sold rather than published): Arkansas, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Mississippi, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Vermont. Some counties elsewhere publish nothing or charge for it (Nassau and most of Suffolk County, New York, for example). If your address isn't found, the ZIP result is still your best guide. Coverage is being extended.
  • Records are properties, not apartments. An apartment building appears once, under its own address, and some records carry a bare parcel number instead of a street address.
  • Assessor years can be wrong. A few sources record the year of a major renovation rather than the original construction, and typos exist. Where a result looks off, the parcel number lets you check the county's record directly.
  • It says nothing about the paint itself, about water pipes, or about the soil. It tells you whether the home is from the era when lead paint was the norm.

For clinics and health departments

A prenatal or well-child visit is the best moment to catch an old house, before the child is exposed rather than after a blood test shows it. The lookup takes about ten seconds on a phone, needs no login, and can be run with the family's address at the visit. A pre-1978 result is a reason to order the blood lead test, to hand over the steps above, and to ask about peeling paint and planned renovations.
